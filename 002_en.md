# August 11 2026, I Have Seen Through the Secret of All Spiritual Practice

[back to README](README.md)

Here is the conclusion first:

> **Spiritual practice is not about eliminating desire.**
>
> **It is about giving desire a way to move that does not create even more desire.**

Many people think spiritual practice means:

* resisting desire
* suppressing impulses
* not being greedy
* not becoming attached
* not getting angry
* not controlling others
* eventually becoming “free of desire”

But there is a problem.

**If you desperately want to become a person with no desire, that desperate wanting is itself a desire.**

So pure suppression easily becomes:

> using one desire to suppress another.

The desire has not disappeared.

It has only moved.

---

## Three Basic Drives

I call them:

**sexual drive, possessive desire, and loving desire.**

These terms are easiest to understand through romantic relationships, but they can apply to how a person relates to almost anything.

### Sexual Drive: The Body Wants Energy

This is the body’s basic need for vitality.

Food, sleep, exercise, sex, rest, and stimulation all belong here.

In simple terms:

> **The sexual drive says: I want to feel alive.**

---

### Possessive Desire: I Want Things to Go My Way

This is the desire for control.

I want something.

I want more money.

I want to win.

I want to prove myself.

I want people to like me.

I want events to follow my plan.

Even:

I want to control my emotions.

I want to completely let go.

I want to have no desire at all.

These may look different, but underneath they can all mean:

> **I want myself, other people, or the world to become what I want them to be.**

That is possessive desire.

---

### Loving Desire: This Is Good, So I Want to Experience It and Let It Exist

Loving desire is not possession.

You see a beautiful flower.

You do not have to pick it.

You love someone.

They do not have to belong to you.

You understand something.

The understanding itself can be enough.

You create something.

You may simply want to bring it into existence.

In simple terms:

> **Loving desire says: this is good; I want to experience it, and I am willing to let that goodness continue.**

It does not require control.

---

# Spiritual Practice Mainly Deals With Possessive Desire

The hardest kind of desire is often not physical need.

It is possessive desire.

Because possessive desire has one major feature:

> **the more you satisfy it, the more it grows.**

You make one hundred thousand and start wanting one million.

You gain someone’s love and begin fearing that you will lose it.

You gain status and want more.

You learn something and want to prove how much you know.

Even spiritual practice can become:

> I must become enlightened.

> I must be more awakened than other people.

> I must become someone without desire.

Then practice itself becomes another object of desire.

---

# Suppression Usually Does Not Solve the Problem

Suppose someone wants to control other people.

They realize this is bad.

So they force themselves:

> I must not control other people.

On the surface, they have become restrained.

But if they spend every day aggressively controlling themselves so that they will not control anyone else, the structure of control is still there.

The target has simply changed from others to themselves.

This is the basic problem:

> **Desire has not disappeared. It has only changed objects.**

---

# This Is Substitution

You cannot get love, so you chase money.

You stop chasing money, so you chase status.

You stop chasing status, so you chase knowledge.

You stop chasing knowledge, so you chase spiritual achievement.

Eventually:

> I must become truly enlightened.

It looks more and more elevated.

But if the structure underneath is still:

> **I must obtain something before I can be satisfied,**

then nothing fundamental has changed.

This is substitution.

The object changes.

The desire does not.

---

# Real Practice Is Redirection

Redirection means:

> **The desire is still there, but you let it move into something that does not create even more desire.**

You want to control the world.

You can observe it instead.

You desperately want to understand everything.

You can investigate.

You want to express yourself.

You can create.

You love beautiful things.

You can appreciate them and create more beauty.

What you do is not the main issue.

The real question is:

> **After doing it, do you want to possess more, or can you simply experience more?**

If afterward you still think:

I need more.

I need to win.

I need to prove myself.

I need to control the outcome.

Then possessive desire is still multiplying.

But if afterward you simply:

saw,

understood,

experienced,

created,

and moved on,

then the desire was expressed without reproducing itself.

That is what makes practice work.

---

# This Is What “Letting Go” Means

It does not mean wanting nothing.

It means:

> **You can want something without absolutely needing to get it.**

It does not mean having no emotions.

It means:

> **An emotion can appear without automatically turning into action.**

It does not mean you stop loving beautiful things.

It means:

> **You can love something without needing to own it.**

It does not mean the desire for control completely disappears.

It means:

> **The desire for control appears, but it no longer controls you.**

That is why a deeply practiced person may look calm, restrained, and unattached.

It seems as if desire has disappeared.

More accurately:

> **Desire is still there, but it no longer keeps creating more desire.**

---

# Why Practice Looks Like “Desirelessness”

Normally, possessive desire works like this:

> want → get → want more → get more

There is no natural end.

Practice changes the loop:

> want → notice → express differently → end

No new “I must have this” automatically follows.

The more often this loop is broken, the weaker possessive desire becomes in everyday behavior.

Eventually, someone may appear almost desireless.

Not because desire has been destroyed.

But because it has **stopped reproducing itself**.

---

# Spiritual Practice Is Not Just “Holding It In”

Holding back can be useful.

You want to explode in anger, so you stop yourself.

You want to possess something, so you do not act immediately.

That can prevent damage.

But if your whole life becomes nothing but restraint, the deeper problem remains.

The real change is:

> **you slowly stop needing control as the main way to express yourself.**

Before, you had to win.

Later, you can simply participate.

Before, you had to be recognized.

Later, you can simply do something well.

Before, you had to own.

Later, you can simply appreciate.

Before, you had to change other people.

Later, you can understand them while still choosing your own path.

That is when practice starts working at a deeper level.

---

# Practice Does Not Turn You Into Someone Without Desire

It changes:

> **your relationship with desire.**

At first:

**desire appears, and you follow it.**

Later:

**desire appears, and you see it.**

Later:

**desire appears, and you can choose where it goes.**

Eventually:

**desire comes, and desire goes.**

No need to suppress it.

No need to satisfy it.

---

# This Is Not a Rejection of Traditional Practice

Buddhism, Daoism, Zen, yoga, meditation, and other traditions all contain practical methods developed over long periods of time.

This explanation is not intended to replace them.

Knowing the principle does not mean you no longer need practice.

Knowing how exercise works does not give you muscle.

Knowing how swimming works does not make you a swimmer.

The same applies here.

This framework only tries to do one thing:

> **explain ancient practices in language that modern people can understand more easily.**

Then, when you practice, you can better ask:

Am I actually practicing,

or merely suppressing myself?

Have I really let go,

or have I simply found something new to cling to?

Have I stopped controlling,

or am I just controlling myself even harder?

---

# Why Practice Works Differently for Different People

Everyone is different.

Bodies differ.

Experiences differ.

Temperaments differ.

Ways of thinking differ.

The direction in which desire tends to flow also differs.

So the same practice can affect two people very differently.

Someone may change quickly.

Someone else may spend decades repeating the same pattern.

Someone may even become more attached through practice.

That is normal.

Understanding the principle does not guarantee rapid progress.

Its value is simpler:

> **it helps you waste less time going in the wrong direction.**

The earlier you know what practice is actually changing, the less likely you are to mistake suppression for practice, substitution for letting go, or a new obsession for awakening.

---

# The Whole Idea in One Sentence

> **Spiritual practice is not about eliminating desire. It is about letting desire exist without allowing it to keep creating more desire.**

Even more simply:

> **At first: “I must get it.”**
>
> **Later: “I can want it without needing to get it.”**

And eventually:

> **“I can love, experience, and create without needing to possess.”**

That is, in my view, the simplest underlying principle behind spiritual practice.
